## Scope

This record covers category D at the level of detection, statute, charge and
enforcement outcome. The conduct is described in the sources linked
below.

## How the case surfaced

PicsArt, an online photo-editing platform, flagged the account and images Fairchild created and
reported to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children
(NCMEC). Then, NCMEC passed the information to the Colorado Internet Crimes Against
Children Task Force and the Boulder County Digital Forensics Lab took over the
investigation. A search warrant was served at Daniel Fairchild's home on
January 15, 2026. He turned himself in on an arrest warrant on January 30, 2026 and was
booked into the Boulder County Jail with bond set at $50,000.

It is worth noting that local authorities detected or victim reported the incident prior to PicsArt alerted. PicsArt content moderation system made the
first detection, while a nonprofit clearinghouse raised the case to the law enforcement. Boulder County described this case as one of its forensics lab's first full
investigations involving AI.

## The statute

Every criminal count is under Colorado state law. Eleven counts of sexual exploitation of a
child by producing sexually exploitative material under C.R.S. section
18-6-403(3)(b), a class 3 felony. Eleven counts of possession under section
18-6-403(3)(b.5), a class 5 felony. One count under section 18-6-403(5.5)(a),
the extraordinary risk enhancer for material involving a child under twelve.

Federal charges were not applied in this case and none of the statutes was laid out for
artificial intelligence. The provisions used in this case predate generative
image tools by a wide margin.

## Altered images

The sheriff's office claims that pictures of real children were altered
using AI. The distinction of whether images were created or altered carries legal weight 
in the US. Wholly synthetic depictions occupy contested constitutional ground, which is why the federal
provision is framed around obscenity rather than considering the
images as contraband outright. Material derived from identifiable real
children does not raise that problem at all.

Whether prosecutors are more willing to charge altered-image cases than wholly
synthetic ones is a question this record cannot answer alone. It is
flagged because it is testable and the distinction is doing more work in these
prosecutions than the coverage suggests.
