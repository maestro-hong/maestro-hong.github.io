## Scope of this entry

This record covers category D at the level of detection, statute, charge and
enforcement outcome. The conduct is described in the sources linked
below. It is not reproduced here, and nothing about how the material was
produced is set out.

## How the case surfaced

The editing platform, PicsArt, flagged an account generating explicit content. The company notified the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC) of the incident. The case reached Boulder County and the Boulder County Digital
Forensics Lab took the investigation. With the search and arrest warrant, the sheriff's office made the arrest of Daniel Fairchild on 30 January 2026, with bond set at $50,000.

It is worth being precise about the order of events, because it inverts the
usual assumption. No police unit detected this. No victim reported it. A
private company's content moderation system produced a signal, a nonprofit
clearinghouse routed it, and a criminal investigation began at the end of
that chain rather than at the start.

Boulder County described this case as the first full investigations its
forensics lab had run involving generative AI. The detection did not come from the lab. It came to the lab.

## The statute

Every criminal count is Colorado state law. Eleven counts of sexual exploitation of a
child by producing sexually exploitative material under C.R.S. section
18-6-403(3)(b), a class 3 felony. Eleven counts of possession under section
18-6-403(3)(b.5), a class 5 felony. One count under section 18-6-403(5.5)(a),
the extraordinary risk enhancer for material involving a child under twelve.

Neither federal charges nor statutes are written to mandate artificial intelligence. The provisions used in this case predate generative image tools by a wide margin.

## Images altered, not generated

Photographs of real children were altered using generative tools in PicsArt. The material was not created from nothing.

The distinction carries legal weight in the United States. Wholly synthetic
depictions occupy contested constitutional ground, which is why the federal
provision covering them is framed around obscenity rather than treating the
images as contraband outright. Material derived from identifiable real
children does not raise that problem at all.

Whether prosecutors are willing to charge altered-image cases than wholly synthetic ones is a question this record cannot answer. It is flagged because it is testable and the distinction is doing more work in these prosecutions than the coverage
suggests.

## Open

**No disposition.** The arrest was in January 2026. This record has no information pertaining to plea, trial date, dismissal or sentences. Charges are allegations and nothing has been tested in court.

**What nobody reported.** No sources provide information of how many images the accused generated, how the photographs of real children were obtained, whether children were identified, or whether their families were notified. Those are the questions that determine the actual harm in a case similar to this. None of the coverage asked them.

**The platform.** None of the sources confirm whether flagging of Fairchild's act was alerted automatically or manually by PicsArt. It also does not state how long the Fairchild's account operated before it was flagged or whether the platform's terms anticipated this use. A detection pathway that begins with private content moderation deserves more scrutiny than it has received.
