## What happened

Christopher Woolcott, 44, of south-east London, owned shares in Touchstone Exploration, an oil and gas company listed on AIM and the Toronto Stock Exchange. He invented a takeover bid for it. The idea was that a public offer would push the share price up.

The Independent reported that he bought the shares between May and November 2024.

The FCA says the approach came from four false identities Woolcott had invented, among them a private equity partner, a colleague and a solicitor. The letters that went with the bid claimed to come from a bank and a law firm. One said £217 million was held on account, with a £100 million credit facility behind it. The offer itself was £154 million in cash, which the prosecutor said was almost double the going share price.

He posted the documents from a Ryman branch. He also met Touchstone's chief executive at a central London hotel under a false name. According to the Independent, he pressed for the bid to be announced publicly.

Touchstone's executives were not taken in. The prosecutor told the court the chief executive thought the offer was "too good to be true". Woolcott's own barrister called the documents "surprisingly amateurish". The shares he held were worth just under £70,000, and the court heard they would probably have doubled in value had the bid gone public. Any effect on Touchstone's share price is unreported.

## How he was caught

The FCA opened a criminal investigation in March 2025. It worked from trading data, phone records, receipts and CCTV. Three numbers used by supposedly different people turned out to belong to one handset. The regulator also used the voter register and open-source research to narrow the field. A parking payment put Woolcott's car near the Ryman branch. Searches of his home and workplace turned up the phone.

The Independent reported that he first refused to hand over the security codes for phones seized from his home. In an interview with the FCA he later admitted he was behind the bid.

It is not known who first alerted the FCA. The FCA says Touchstone is not under investigation.

## The AI claim

The FCA's sentencing release says Woolcott forged the takeover documents "using Chat GPT". It gives no further detail.

The Independent and Tech Times both report that the prosecutor, Denis Barry, told the court what Woolcott said in his FCA interview. He used ChatGPT for document templates and guidance. He took logos from Google and signed the papers with improvised signatures.

That account came from Woolcott himself, relayed by the prosecutor. The FCA's release on his guilty plea in September did not mention AI; the detail first came out at sentencing.

## Charges and sentence

Woolcott pleaded guilty at Westminster Magistrates' Court on September 10, 2026 to one count of fraud by false representation and three counts of making a false instrument.

On November 10, 2026 Judge Usha Karu at Southwark Crown Court sentenced him to two years in prison, suspended for two years. He must also do 200 hours of unpaid work and pay £4,627.73 towards prosecution costs. The FCA says the judge described the scheme as deliberate, sophisticated and planned. His barrister told the court he had been trying to win back money lost on Touchstone shares and called the plan foolish.

Fraud under the Fraud Act 2006 carries up to 10 years in prison on conviction on indictment. Making a false instrument under the Forgery and Counterfeiting Act 1981 also carries up to 10 years.

Therese Chambers, the FCA's executive director of enforcement and market oversight, said the bid was "a calculated attempt to profit by manipulating the market".
