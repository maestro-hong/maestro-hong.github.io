## What happened

On July 24, 2026, the Justice Department's Criminal Division issued a release announcing that four men had pleaded guilty to wire fraud in a Minnesota Medicaid case. They are Moktar Hassan Aden, 31; Mustafa Dayib Ali, 29; Khalid Ahmed Dayib, 26; and Abdifitah Mohamud Mohamed, 27, all from the Twin Cities area. Each man pleaded guilty to one count at separate hearings between July 7 and July 23, 2026. The July release says each faces up to 20 years in prison and that no sentencing dates had been set. It does not name the court or the judge.

According to the July release, the four set up Brilliant Minds Services LLC in St. Paul and enrolled it as a Medicaid provider in Minnesota's Housing Stabilization Services program, which is now defunct. They submitted thousands of claims, either for services that were never provided or with significantly inflated amounts, and signed up about 350 people. The July release puts the amount taken from Minnesota Medicaid between April 2022 and April 2025 at about $2.2 million. The FBI, IRS Criminal Investigation and the Health and Human Services Office of Inspector General investigated. The July release does not say whether the company itself was charged or entered a plea.

On September 18, 2025, the U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Minnesota issued a release announcing wire fraud charges against eight people in a first wave of separate Housing Stabilization cases. The September release lists the four men under Brilliant Minds Services LLC, in United States v. Moktar Aden et al., 25-cr-349, and puts the claims the company submitted at about $2.3 million. The July release gives no case number.

## The AI claim

The July release's subtitle says the defendants "Used Artificial Intelligence to Fabricate Records", and a summary line under the headline says they did so "when questioned by insurance companies". The account of the case in the body names no insurer. The body says that when the defendants were asked to provide supporting documentation for their claims, they "fabricated records using ChatGPT in an attempt to conceal their theft". ChatGPT appears once, in the sentence after the one saying the four signed up about 350 people. It also says the case "highlights a burgeoning trend" of AI use in fraud schemes targeting health care programs.

The July release does not say AI was used to submit claims, enroll people or move money. The only use of AI it describes is the fabricated records.

The July release does not describe the records, say when they were made or say whether anyone accepted them. It attributes the names, ages and company details to "court documents", but the ChatGPT sentence carries no such attribution. The September release does not mention AI.

KTTC's subheading says the defendants "used AI-generated fake records to enroll about 350 people". KTTC's story attributes that claim to the Justice Department. The July release does not link the records to enrollment.
