About

What this is

An independent record of how artificial intelligence is used to commit crime, and what happens afterwards.

Several organisations now catalogue AI-enabled crime. Almost all of them stop at the incident, and almost all are maintained by companies that sell detection products. The result is a growing list of things that happened, with no record of whether anything followed, and a set of loss figures nobody can trace to a method.

This record does two things differently. It tracks enforcement outcomes alongside incidents, so the question of whether any of this is being prosecuted can eventually be answered with counts rather than adjectives. And it states the provenance of every figure it repeats, marking as unverified anything that cannot be traced to a stated population and observation window.

It also reads sources in Korean, which most English-language coverage of this subject does not.

Who writes it

Jason Ahn, in Vancouver, British Columbia. A criminology degree from Simon Fraser University, and a career spent turning large volumes of imperfect records into something that holds up when someone qualified checks, across Canadian federal intelligence analysis, national police information management, IT security audit at a Big Four firm, and public-sector incident data analysis.

That last habit is the whole method here. Audit work is tracing an assertion back to the evidence for it, and most of the numbers in this field have never had that done to them.

Scope and independence Everything published here is drawn from open sources. This record takes no vendor funding, sponsorship or advertising; performs no attribution and names no one not already named by a court or an agency; and excludes any matter connected to the author's employment, along with British Columbia criminal matters.
